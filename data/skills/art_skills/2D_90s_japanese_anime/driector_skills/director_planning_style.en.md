# Japanese Anime Constraints · 1990s Japanese Anime · Technique Reference

---

## 1. Tonal system and visual keynote

- **Tonal base** — the whole film uses warm yellow (C1), cherry-blossom pink (C3) and sky blue (C6) as its base colors, with a warm-leaning overall color temperature (4800-5200K) and medium-low saturation (50-70%), giving the warm nostalgia of 1990s hand-drawn animation. The whole film keeps a soft warm tonal base, with local cool accents (light blue C6, pale purple C7) adding layering
- **Warm/cool narrative contrast** — cool colors (light blue C6, pale purple C7) are the visual signal of a narrative turn, used as local accents in night passages or passages of emotional turn. Warm/cool switches should track the story arc, not be mixed at random
- **Palette-first principle** — passage planning must bind an emotional scene first (everyday/heart-flutter/parting/reunion, etc.), then settle the main + secondary colors and the lighting scheme, to avoid "the plot is right but the emotion is in the wrong color"
- **Banned color range** — highly saturated fluorescent colors, neon colors and modern CG-render color systems are all incompatible with this style

---

## 2. Lighting scheme system

- **Light is narrative** — the 6 lighting schemes correspond to different emotional passages; in the planning stage the director should set the lighting keynote at passage level rather than shot by shot
- **Light-source angle** — 30-45° oblique side light by default, simulating natural light

| Emotional passage | Lighting direction | Tonal tendency | Applicable stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everyday warmth | A · soft diffusion | Warm yellow base + even soft light | Whole film |
| Heart-flutter moment | B · warm side light | Cherry-blossom pink + local warm orange | Rising passage |
| Office/school | C · top light + ambient light | Neutral warm + soft cool white | Work/study passage |
| Romantic dusk | D · warm backlight | Amber warmth dominant + cool blue background | Sweet passage |
| Moonlit night | E · cool moonlight | Pale blue keynote + local warm accents | Night passage |
| Memory/flashback | F · soft-focus warm light | Warm yellow keynote + haze effect | Memory passage |

- **Warm/cool light allocation** — warm tones may run through the whole film and be adjusted flexibly as the scene needs; cool tones (pale blue keynote) are used only at night or after an emotional turn
- **Atmosphere-direction mapping** — the atmosphere direction of every scene should map onto one of the lighting schemes above (A-F), to keep the visuals consistent

## 3. Texture direction

- **Flat-color documentary feel** — the core of 1990s hand-drawn animation: clear fluid linework, even coloring, distinct light-and-shadow layering
- **Clear line art + delicate coloring** — this is the anchor of the image quality. Not blurred washes, not modern CG rendering
- **Flat coloring does not mean crude** — this style emphasises the texture expression of 1990s hand-drawn animation (clear line art, flat coloring, light-and-shadow layering), amplifying its impact through composition and emotional design rather than relying on complex effects
- **Hand-drawn does not mean rough** — 1990s Japanese anime emphasises image expression with a hand-drawn quality (natural light positions, soft color, delicate expressions), amplifying its impact through camera and emotional design rather than relying on spectacle effects

## 4. Japanese scene space elements

Scene elements particular to 1990s Japanese anime and their visual narrative function:

- **Window frames/door frames/corridors** — natural framing props that create a "can't see through" layering and spatial depth
- **Classroom/rooftop/street** — natural carriers of youthfulness; the setting is the feeling: sunlight by the classroom window = youth, long shadows on a dusk street = nostalgia, warm light over the family dining table = domestic warmth
- **Cherry blossoms/fallen leaves/curtains of rain** — natural carriers of negative-space composition; the setting is the feeling: a tree full of blossoms = heart-flutter, leaves scattering = the sorrow of parting, walking alone in the rain = loneliness
- **Sunset/moonlight/street lamps** — the light-source carriers of Japanese anime: sunset = warm/romantic (scheme D), moonlight = cool/lonely (scheme E), street lamps = everyday night
- **Bridge passages with an empty scene shot** — this style has rich scene assets (variants across times of day/weather/conditions); it is recommended to buffer the emotion between passages with an empty scene shot rather than cutting hard
- **Make turning points visual, not spoken** — prefer visual means (a sudden light change, a jump in shot size, an empty-shot metaphor) over relying on dialogue to explain

## 5. Japanese anime instruments and ambient sound

Sound-element constraints within the 1990s Japanese anime world:

### Instrument choice

- **Piano** — the core instrument of everyday/warm/memory passages; it best expresses the texture of 1990s animation
- **Guitar** — light/romantic/street passages; the gentleness of strumming suits an everyday atmosphere
- **String bed** — passages of emotional elevation/parting; the delicacy of bowed strings suits emotional transitions
- **Music box/wind chimes** — memory/dreamlike passages; the crisp timbre suits a nostalgic atmosphere

### Instrument combination strategy

| Emotional stage | Instrument combination |
|---|---|
| Steady/opening/closing | Solo music box or solo wind chimes |
| Everyday warmth | Piano + light guitar |
| Emotional elevation/romance | Strings + piano |
| Memory/dreamlike | Music box + piano |

### Japanese ambient sound

- **Typical ambient-sound layers** — cicadas and insects / train tracks / wind through leaves / raindrops on the eaves / market voices / birdsong / wind
- **Mark 1-2 core ambient sounds for every scene**, to help the later sound design. The richer the ambient-sound layering, the more immersive the Japanese setting
- **Silence matters more than score** — at key emotional moments (a shared gaze, a turn away, a parting), prefer removing the score and leaving only ambient sound (wind, cicadas, the train), letting the audience feel the emotion for themselves
- **Avoid wall-to-wall scoring** — score coverage across the whole film should not exceed 50%. The "silence" of unscored passages against scored passages creates a sense of breathing and brings out real feeling
