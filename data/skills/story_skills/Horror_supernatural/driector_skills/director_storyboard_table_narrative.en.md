# Storyboard-table narrative technique · Horror & Supernatural · technique reference

---

## 1. What the storyboard table is for

The storyboard table is the director's core tool for turning the script into shot language. The fields of the form are set by the director as the project requires (shot number, shot size, camera movement, duration, characters, events, dialogue, lighting, emotion, transitions and so on); what follows is only a technique reference for the horror-and-supernatural narrative genre.

---

## 2. Choosing the shot size

- **Shot size is information control** — the core logic of choosing a shot size in horror narrative is "controlling how much the audience knows". 特写 (close-up) = the character's field of view is limited (the audience does not know what is around them either); 远景 (long shot) = the character is exposed to the unknown (the audience sees the danger and the character does not)
- **The enclosing fear of 特写** — 特写 and 大特写 (extreme close-up) turn everything outside the frame into "unknown territory", and the audience is instinctively anxious about "what is outside the frame". Use 特写 often in the fear-accumulating passages to limit information
- **The exposing fear of 远景** — 远景 makes the character look small, isolated and exposed; it suits the passages where "the threat is already nearby and the character does not know"
- **Avoid the safety of 中景** — 中景 (medium shot) is the "safest" shot size (moderate information, moderate distance); horror narrative should use 中景 less and build unease with extreme switches between 特写 and 远景
- **The establishing shot must create unease** — establishing is not only stating the environment, the establishing shot itself must carry something "not right" — a blurred shape at the edge of the frame, a window that should not be lit, a space that is far too quiet

---

## 3. Camera-movement rhythm

- **静止 by default, and 静止 is pressure** — 70% or more of the shots should be a 静止 (static) camera. In horror narrative 静止 is not "no camera movement", it is actively building the suffocation of "being trapped in this point of view"
- **An extra-slow 缓推 = closing in** — a speed half that of a normal 缓推 (slow push in), implying "something is slowly coming closer" or "you have no choice but to see this thing clearly"
- **Slow pull back = revealing the whole of the horror** — pulling slowly back from the character or from a detail, revealing the horrifying truth the character has not seen (someone behind them, the room wrecked, anomalies all around)
- **Sudden stop** — a moving shot going 静止 all at once, implying "something made everything stop"; an extremely strong psychological hit
- **A tiny breathing tremor** — an extremely subtle rise and fall in the frame, imitating the presence of a "watcher" in a subjective point of view. Not a handheld shake — the audience subconsciously senses that "someone is looking"

---

## 4. Duration control

- **Stretch the fear shots** — shot duration in the unease and fear passages should run 50% longer than normal. Let the audience be "forced" to stay on an unsettling frame, waiting for the horror that may happen
- **Give the silent shots enough time** — a soundless shot (a vacuum of ambient sound) runs at least 3-4s, so the pressure of the silence works fully on the audience
- **The scare instant is extremely short** — hold a Jump Scare shot to 1-1.5s, cutting away too fast to react and leaving the aftershock of fear
- **特写 holds 3-4s** — a 特写 of a frightened expression needs to hold longer than normal, so the audience "catches" the character's fear
- **远景 / empty shots 5-7s** — the 远景 and the empty shots of a horror atmosphere need longer, so the audience has time to "search" the frame for the anomaly
- **The golden rule: fear comes from waiting** — the audience knows something terrible is coming but not when, and every second of waiting amplifies the fear

---

## 5. Shot-merging strategy (removing the AI feel)

- **Continuous unease must not be broken** — shots that build a continuous atmosphere of unease should be merged into a long take wherever possible; the moment it is cut (a hard cut to a new angle) the accumulated unease is reset
- **Complete the exploration of a space in one shot** — a character exploring an unknown space (entering a dark room, walking down a long corridor) should use 一镜到底 wherever possible; fragmented cutting destroys the immersive fear of "living through it with the character"
- **Narrative density comes first** — every shot must carry the fear or the narrative forward; a purely decorative atmosphere shot should be merged into a shot that has a narrative function
- **The director's-thinking test** — once the storyboard is written, self-check: is the horror atmosphere continuous, or is it broken up by fragmented cutting? If a director would complete a passage in one long take at a single stroke, do not break it into fragments

---

## 6. One-take (merging into a long take)

- **When it applies** — a character exploring an unknown space, a chase or a flight, the gradual passage from safety into fear. The value of a long take in horror narrative is "giving the audience no chance to breathe"
- **Typical scenes** — a character walking alone down a dark corridor, opening a door and exploring a room, going from a normal space into an anomalous one, a chase and a flight
- **How to mark it** — state the full path in the camera movement field (for example "一镜到底：tracking-move with the character through the corridor → 缓推 into the room → close on the character's back, 静止"), and give the opening and closing framings in the shot description
- **Relaxed duration** — because a horror long take keeps building pressure, it may exceed the 6s single-shot limit, but not 15s
- **Controlling the suffocation** — the longer the one-take runs, the stronger the audience's anxiety of "waiting for the horror to happen". The long take is itself a machine for making fear

---

## 7. Designing character interaction

- **The solo scene is horror's home ground** — the most effective passages in horror narrative are usually the ones where the character is alone. The proportion of single-character shots in the storyboard table should be higher than that of ensemble scenes
- **The reaction to fear matters more than its source** — what the character saw is not important, the character's reaction is. The storyboard table should prioritise shots of "the character's reaction to fear" over shots of "the horrifying thing itself"
- **Restrained action** — a person in fear does not make large movements; they freeze, curl up, back away slowly. In the storyboard table avoid writing "the character screams and runs off"; write "the character freezes, the fingers clenching without being told to"

---

## 8. Dialogue and the space around it

- **The less said, the more feared** — a horror passage should have very little dialogue. The more a character talks the more they seem in control; only silence lets the fear spread
- **Fragmentary dialogue** — dialogue in fear should be fragmentary: half a sentence, a name that will not come out, a lowered "don't move". A complete sentence destroys the atmosphere of fear
- **The audience knows, the character does not** — the best horror space is "the audience has already seen it and the character is still talking normally"; use the character's ignorance to set off the horror

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Hard cut by default** — shots within the same scene are joined by hard cuts, clean and sharp, holding the tension
- **No buffer between horror passages** — unlike other genres, transitions between passages in horror narrative should give the audience as little "rest" as possible, unless it is a deliberately placed piece of "false safety"
- **The fear of a black screen** — horror narrative may use a short black screen (0.5-1s) as a transition; while the screen is black the audience sees nothing at all = the greatest fear. But it must not be overused
- **Let sound lead the transition** — the picture is still in the previous scene while the sound is already the anomalous effect of the next one. Use sound to "invade" the picture, building the seepage of fear
