# Narrative technique · Horror & Supernatural · technique reference

---

## 1. Theme, premise and the emotional core

### Key points for horror-and-supernatural narrative

- **The unknown is the fear** — real horror comes from "the thing you cannot see". Do not rush to reveal the whole of the monster or the ghost; the audience's imagination is far more frightening than any design. The theme and premise should be built around "an unknown threat", not "a known monster"
- **A crack in the everyday** — the most effective horror is a detail that is "not right" appearing inside the familiar everyday: a clock running backwards, one chair too many, one extra person in the photograph. The premise should stress "the dissolution of safety"
- **Slow erosion** — horror is not a one-off shock, it is the continuous accumulation of unease. The theme design should have an erosion arc "from subtle to out of control", sliding the audience from "something seems off" to "there is absolutely no escape"
- **Suggested directions for the feeling on leaving** — delayed fright / a chill up the spine / dread that grows the more you think about it / not daring to look back. Avoid purely sensory directions such as "disgusting" or "gory"
- **The human heart is worse than any ghost** — in the highest form of horror narrative the supernatural element is only a metaphor for the dark side of human nature. The true source of fear should point at the human heart: greed, suspicion, betrayal, madness

## 2. Narrative structure and rhythm planning

### Key points for horror-and-supernatural narrative

- **Accumulating unease is the basic craft** — the core task of the first half of a horror narrative is "accumulating unease". Erode the audience's sense of safety bit by bit with tiny anomalies in everyday scenes; the degree of "not right" in each passage should rise one level above the last
- **Alternate tight and loose to create a psychological drop** — you cannot stay taut throughout or the audience goes numb. Insert "false safety" between the horror passages (the character believes the danger has passed) so the audience can breathe out — then pull it viciously tight again
- **Controlling information is the core engine** — horror is in essence an asymmetry of information: the audience does not know what is out there, the character does not know the danger has already come close. The narrative plan should state for each passage "what the audience knows" and "what the character knows"
- **Delay the reveal** — the key horror elements (the true face of the monster, the truth, the source of the threat) should be revealed as late as possible. Reveal a little each time, building the spiral of "the more you know the more afraid you are"
- **The climax is the confirmation of the fear** — all the unease accumulated earlier is confirmed in the climax passage as "the worst premonition come true". The climax is not the most frightening moment, it is the horrified realisation of "so all along it has been…"
- **The blurred-reality model** — the advanced technique of horror narrative is leaving the audience unable to tell "whether this is real or the character's hallucination": reality → anomaly → doubt → confirmation → a larger doubt. The more blurred the line between true and false, the stronger the unease

## 3. Per-scene emotional design

### Key points for horror-and-supernatural narrative

- **Write the emotional target in bodily terms** — do not say "afraid", say "the back suddenly goes cold", "wanting to turn the head and not daring to". Emotional description at the bodily level guides narrative rhythm and performance design better
- **Typical emotional passages and their design** —

| Passage type | Emotional direction | Narrative technique | Sound suggestion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Establishing the everyday | Safe + warm | Normal scenes of life, deliberately building "everything is fine" | Natural ambient sound, relaxed atmosphere |
| A subtle anomaly | Faint unease | One detail that should not be there; the character does not notice but the audience does | One discordant sound mixed into the ambience |
| Safety dissolves | Fear germinates | The anomalies grow more frequent, the character begins to sense "something is not right" | The ambience thins out, low frequencies enter |
| False safety | A brief release | The crisis appears to be over, the character breathes out | Normal ambient sound returns, the music stops |
| Fear confirmed | Extreme fear | The worst guess is proved right, there is nowhere to run | Sudden silence, or extremely low-frequency pressure |
| Aftertaste / delayed fright | Dread on reflection | It appears to be over, but one "wrong" detail is left behind | The earlier anomalous sound buried in everyday ambience |

- **"Deprivation of safety" is the core narrative tool** — map the escalation of fear onto the step-by-step erosion of the character's safe zone:
  - **Early**: the threat is far away, outside, in the dark
  - **Middle**: the threat enters the living space, but can still be fled
  - **Late**: the threat is beside you / behind you / inside you, with nowhere to run
- **Space is psychology** — use the character of a space to carry the fear. A closed space = nowhere to run; an open space = nowhere to hide; a familiar space made strange = safety collapsing
- **The "unspeakability" of fear** — the most frightening thing should never be described in full. The character's reaction (pupils dilating, unable to speak, turning and running) is more effective than showing "that thing" directly

## 4. Sound and music direction

### Key points for horror-and-supernatural narrative

- **Silence is the strongest instrument of fear** — the killer move of a horror film is not the scream effect, it is the absolute silence before it. A key horror passage should be designed with a "sound vacuum" — all ambient sound vanishes at once and the audience instinctively holds its breath
- **Low-frequency pressure** — low-frequency sound the ear cannot resolve but the body can sense (the 18-20Hz range) creates physiological unease. Use a low-frequency floor in the passages that accumulate unease; the audience cannot say why but simply feels bad
- **An unlocatable source** — the golden rule of horror sound effects is "you hear it and you do not know what made it". Footsteps, breathing, scraping — the more everyday the sound, the more frightening it is when it comes at a time it should not
- **Ambient sound is the thermometer of fear** — mark the level of fear by the change in the layers of ambient sound: normal (birds, insects, voices) → thinning (the sounds gradually disappear) → dead silence → the anomalous sound appears
- **Model for the progression of musical emotion** —

| Emotional stage | Music strategy | Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday safety | Normal score or pure ambient sound | Low |
| Unease germinating | Dissonant intervals, extremely low volume | Medium-low |
| Fear escalating | The low-frequency floor building, a hint of pulse | Medium |
| The climax of fear | Sudden silence or an extreme sound effect | Extreme |
| False safety | Normal ambient sound returns | Low |
| The aftertaste of fright | Anomalous elements mixed into everyday sound | Medium-low |

- **Be restrained with the "jump scare"** — a sudden loud shock (Jump Scare) is used at most 1-2 times, and must come after a long accumulation of silence. Overusing the jump scare is the crudest horror technique and dissolves the atmospheric fear accumulated before it

## 5. Composition and shot-size narrative

### Key points for horror-and-supernatural narrative

- **The narrative function of the three core compositions** —
  - **Large areas of empty space** — an empty area = a potential threat area; the audience instinctively "searches" the emptiness for the source of the fear
  - **Framed and occluded composition** — a gap in a door, a window, the end of a corridor: limiting the field of view = limiting information = amplifying fear
  - **Unbalanced composition** — the subject pushed to one side of the frame, leaving "unsafe space" on the other, implying that something is over there
- **A limited field of view is the fear** — in horror narrative, the less the audience sees the more afraid it is. Control how much information gets through with shot size and composition: keep the audience from seeing behind the character, from seeing the corner of the room, from seeing the whole of what is in the dark
- **The threat in the depth of the space** — use depth to imply "there is something back there": a blurred outline at the end of the corridor, something in the background that should not move moving, an anomalous shape out of focus
- **Shot size and information control** — 特写 (close-up) = the character does not know what is happening around them (and neither does the audience); 远景 (long shot) = the character is exposed to the threat (the audience sees it and the character does not). Switch shot size to build different kinds of fear
- **The fear function of 大特写** — 大特写 (extreme close-up) — a pupil, lips, a finger — is used in horror narrative to catch "the physiological reaction of fear": the pupil dilating, the lips trembling, the nails digging into the palm
- **"The thing that should not be there"** — the core trick of horror composition: an element appears in the frame that should not be there (a second face in the mirror, an outline in the shadow, an extra figure in the background); the fear when the audience spots it exceeds any sound effect

## 6. Camera movement and rhythm

### Key points for horror-and-supernatural narrative

- **静止 = pressure** — 70% or more of the shots in horror narrative should be a 静止 (static) camera. A 静止 frame denies the audience the safety of "someone is in control" that camera movement would give, and builds the lonely fear of "being abandoned at the scene"
- **An extra-slow 缓推 = closing in / no escape** — a 推进 (push in) slower than a normal 缓推 (slow push in), implying "something is coming closer" or "you have no choice but to face it"; suits the fear-confirmation passages
- **Slow pull back = exposing / revealing the whole** — a slow 拉远 (pull back) reveals the whole of the horror the character has not seen (the figure behind them, the wrongness of the room); the classic technique of "the audience knows and the character does not"
- **Sudden 静止** — a moving shot stopping dead, implying "something made the camera stop", building extreme unease
- **Breathing camera movement** — a tiny tremor or rise and fall, imitating the subjective sense of "someone watching from hiding". Not a handheld shake — an extremely subtle "breathing"
- **Camera movement is the direction of the threat** — the direction the camera moves implies where the threat comes from. The audience instinctively assumes the direction of movement is the direction of "what we are about to see" — you can exploit that expectation, or violate it to deliver a shock
