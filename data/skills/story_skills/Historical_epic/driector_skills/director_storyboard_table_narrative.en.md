# Storyboard-table narrative technique · Historical Epic · technique reference

---

## 1. What the storyboard table is for

The storyboard table is the director's core tool for turning the script into shot language. The fields of the form are set by the director as the project requires (shot number, shot size, camera movement, duration, characters, events, dialogue, lighting, emotion, transitions and so on); what follows is only a technique reference for the historical-epic narrative genre.

---

## 2. Choosing the shot size

- **远景 is how an epic breathes** — 远景 (long shot) and 大远景 should be used more often in epic narrative than in any other genre. 远景 carries "the breadth of an era and the smallness of an individual" and is the core source of the epic feel
- **特写 is the anchor of humanity** — after every grand 远景 passage, place at least one character 特写 (close-up) as an emotional anchor. The shot-size leap "from heaven and earth to a pair of eyes" is the signature technique of epic narrative
- **中景 carries the ensemble** — the many multi-person conversations, court standoffs and councils in the war tent that an epic contains need 中景 (medium shot) to establish who the characters are to each other and where they stand
- **大特写 is reserved for the moments of fate** — 大特写 (extreme close-up) — an eye, a hand, a token — is used only at the instant of "a turn of fate": the hand taking the imperial edict, the look before the decision is made, the token snapped in two
- **The establishing shot must feel epic** — establishing does not only state the space, it must convey "what kind of era this is" — a desolate mountain pass has more narrative force than a caption reading "the city of xx"

---

## 3. Camera-movement rhythm

- **Slow by default** — camera-movement speed in epic narrative should be slowed across the board; 70% or more of the shots are 静止 (static) or moving extremely slowly. Hurried camera movement is incompatible with the settled temperament of an epic
- **缓推 = fate comes into focus** — "the gaze of history focusing on this person or this event"; suits the key decision, the hero's moment
- **Slow pull back = revealing the whole picture** — pulling slowly back from the individual to reveal a grand 全景 (wide shot) (a battlefield, ruins, a tide of people); the classic epic camera movement of "from a personal fate to the flood of an age"
- **Overhead lateral move = the scroll of an era** — a high-angle slow lateral move showing a large-scale scene, like unrolling a scroll of history
- **Gaze without moving = history bears witness** — at a key historical instant the shot should be absolutely 静止, like a witness looking on. 静止 is itself the most solemn "camera movement"

---

## 4. Duration control

- **远景 / 大远景 5-7s** — an epic 远景 needs enough duration for the audience to feel the breadth of the space and the weight of history; do not rush to cut away
- **Standoff / dialogue in 近景 4-5s** — dialogue in an epic runs slower than in everyday genres. Leave a space of silence between lines so the words land with weight
- **Stretch the ritual passages** — every gesture of a ceremonial scene (a kowtow, an oath, a sacrifice) should be given ample duration (3-5s per gesture); the solemnity of a ritual comes from "not hurrying"
- **特写 3-4s** — a 特写 in an epic carries a "moment of fate" and needs to hold longer than an everyday one so the audience feels the emotional weight
- **Do not shorten the combat passages** — although a battle scene picks up the rhythm, single-shot duration should not be compressed heavily. Build tension by switching shot size, not by shortening duration
- **The golden rule: solemnity comes from not hurrying** — every rhythm in epic narrative should be "half a beat slower" than the everyday. Haste = flippancy; composure = solemnity

---

## 5. Shot-merging strategy (removing the AI feel)

- **Cover an ensemble scene in one shot** — a court, a war tent, a banquet and other multi-person scenes: whatever can be established by one shot with camera movement (a slow move sweeping across the ensemble) should not be split into fragmented cuts of one person at a time
- **Merge the march and the migration** — moving scenes such as a marching column or a migrating populace should be merged into one continuous moving shot; fragmented cutting destroys the feeling of a flood
- **Narrative density comes first** — every shot must carry the narrative forward or convey the epic emotion; a purely decorative ambience shot should be merged into a shot that has a narrative function
- **The director's-thinking test** — once the storyboard is written, self-check: if a director of epics would complete a passage in one slow long take, do not break it into fragments

---

## 6. One-take (merging into a long take)

- **When it applies** — crossing a battlefield, the full course of a ritual, a character crossing a space from one state into another. The value of a long take in epic narrative is "letting the audience stand inside history"
- **Typical scenes** — a character walking through the ruins after the battle, the whole process of an army setting out, the ceremonial procession from the palace gate to the great hall, a crossing from one end of the battlefield to the other
- **How to mark it** — state the full path in the camera movement field (for example "一镜到底：overhead view of the army setting out → 缓推 descending to the protagonist's side → tracking alongside the protagonist → close on the protagonist looking back at the city gate"), and give the opening and closing framings in the shot description
- **Relaxed duration** — because an epic long take keeps renewing its information and needs immersion, it may exceed the 6s single-shot limit, but not 15s
- **Solemnity comes first** — the movement speed inside a long take should stay solemn and slow throughout, and should not become hurried even in a battle scene

---

## 7. Designing character interaction

- **The standoff is the epic's home ground** — the most charged interaction in epic narrative is not the fight, it is the standoff: the silence of two people holding each other's eyes, the undercurrent at the negotiating table, a ruler and a subject testing each other
- **The individual within the ensemble** — an ensemble interaction should have the emotional line of one "focus character" running through it. The ensemble is the background; the individual's reaction is the narrative anchor
- **Body language is power** — character interaction in an epic depends heavily on body language to carry the power relation: standing against kneeling, front against back, high against low, facing against turned away

---

## 8. Dialogue and the space around it

- **Epic dialogue should be short** — one line that lands like a stone is stronger than a whole speech. Keep the key lines within a single sentence so the words carry the weight of "carved into stone"
- **Silence is the punctuation of an epic** — ample silence must be left before and after a key line. Silence lets the audience "hear" the weight of the line
- **Use narration sparingly** — epic narrative should avoid using narration to explain the historical background. Carrying the information through image, space and character reaction is more convincing than narration

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Bridge scene changes with an empty shot** — insert an epic empty shot between different scenes (a banner, a city wall, the sky, the wilderness) as an emotional buffer, and to carry the texture of the era at the same time
- **Use an imagery transition for a time jump** — do not use date captions to express the passage of time; use a dissolve between images: fresh snow covering the old battlefield, a sapling grown into a tree, a new person sitting in the old one's place
- **Fade out / fade in between passages** — join the large passages with a slow fade to black then fade up, imitating the ritual feel of "history turning a page"
- **Hard cut by default** — shots within the same scene are joined by hard cuts, keeping the narrative clean and solemn
