# Storyboard-table narrative technique · Mystery & Thriller · technique reference

---

## 1. What the storyboard table is for

The storyboard table is the director's core tool for turning the script into shot language. The fields of the form are set by the director as the project requires (shot number, shot size, camera movement, duration, characters, events, dialogue, lighting, emotion, transitions and so on); what follows is only a technique reference for the mystery & thriller narrative genre.

---

## 2. Choosing the shot size

- **Shot size is quantity of information** — the core logic of shot size in a mystery storyboard is "how much information do I let the audience see". For every shot, ask first: what should the audience know here? What must be hidden?
- **The selective 特写** — use 特写 (close-up) to force the audience's attention onto a detail (a key, an expression, a line of text), but beware: too obvious a 特写 is the same as drawing an arrow, and the audience will guess it is a clue. The best clue 特写 is "motivated" (the character is looking at it or using it)
- **The concealing 中景** — the core value of 中景 (medium shot) in mystery is "show plenty while hiding the essential". A 中景 on a person states the action but leaves the micro-expression unreadable; a 中景 of a space shows the room but covers the corner
- **The reversal 全景** — 全景 (wide shot) is the most powerful reversal tool in mystery: cutting from a detail to 全景 suddenly reveals "there is someone else in the room", "there is blood on the floor", "the window is open". The information explosion of a 全景 belongs at the instant of the reversal
- **Planting suspense in the establishing shot** — establishing is not only stating the environment; plant one "off" detail into the establishing shot (a figure at a distant window, one extra chair in the yard). Complete establishing plus the seed of suspense in 1-2 shots at most

## 3. Camera-movement rhythm

- **Stable by default, but not rigid** — 50% of shots on a 静止 (static) camera, but key passages may bring in a slight instability (a small handheld amplitude, an extremely slow lateral move) to hint that something is wrong
- **缓推 = discovery / closing in** — pushing slowly towards an object or a person implies "the audience is about to see something important". The slower the 缓推 (slow push in), the stronger the suspense
- **Slow pull back = revelation / the whole** — pulling slowly from a detail out to 全景 reveals the information that was blocked. It is the classic "reversal move" of mystery
- **Lateral move = scanning / searching** — a slow lateral move simulates sweeping the eyes across a space and suits investigation scenes. The audience searches along with the camera, which increases involvement
- **Sudden 静止 = the impact of discovery** — a moving camera stopping abruptly creates a "freeze". Use it when the protagonist suddenly sees something they should not have seen
- **The watching-from-hiding movement** — 缓推 from behind an obstruction, keeping the edge of the obstruction in the foreground, simulating the third-party view of "someone observing from the dark"

---

## 4. Duration control

- **Stretch the suspense shot** — a key "discovery" shot in mystery may run 1-2s longer than normal to give the audience time to realise. A character staring at something for 3s is more unsettling than for 1s
- **Keep everyday shots normal** — shots in the everyday or false-surface passages keep normal durations (2-4s); do not break the disguise with an abnormal duration
- **Stretch before the reversal** — the last shot before the reversal shot should be lengthened appropriately (4-5s) to create the calm before the storm
- **The instant of the reversal is either very short or very long** — the revealing shot of a reversal is either very short (a 1-1.5s flash cut, for impact) or very long (a 5-6s slow reveal, for spreading dread); do not use anything in between
- **The golden 6-second rule still applies** — unless camera movement or new information keeps feeding in, a single shot does not exceed 6s. The "slowness" of mystery is not long shots, it is the swell and fall of the passage rhythm
- **The silent countdown** — a shot of pure silence with 无台词 (no dialogue) and no action is a powerful tool in mystery, but keep it under 4s or the audience will assume a technical fault

## 5. Designing clue shots

- **Do not flag the foreshadowing shot** — the shot that plants a clue must carry no special marking in the storyboard table (avoid the AI feel of "attention! this is a clue!"); let the clue shot look exactly as natural as an ordinary shot
- **Three principles for a clue's appearance** — (1) it has a narrative reason to be there (the character is using it, looking at it, passing it); (2) it is not in the centre of frame (put it at the edge or in the foreground); (3) it gets no abnormal duration (it passes at the normal rhythm)
- **An important clue appears at least twice** — the first time "passed by without meaning", the second time "found to have meaning". Leave enough distance between the two so the audience misses the first
- **The flashback shot changes texture** — memory and flashback shots need a marked visual separation from the present narrative in the storyboard (different colour temperature, different shot-size tendency, different camera-movement speed) so the audience reads the timeline at a glance
- **Prop continuity** — in a mystery storyboard the change in a prop's position is itself narrative: the cup has moved, the vase has been shifted, one photograph is missing. State the exact state of the key props in the shot description

## 6. Strategy for revealing information

- **Small reveals through dialogue, big reveals through picture** — secondary information may be stated through characters talking, but the core reversal must be completed in shot language (revelation in 全景, focus in 特写, flashback assembly)
- **Revelation rhythm: the character first, the audience second** — the classic rhythm of a reversal shot: shoot the change in the character's expression first (shock, fear) → then cut to what the character sees (only now does the audience learn why). The character's reaction preheats the audience's emotion
- **Or the reverse: the audience first, the character second** — the audience sees the truth first (there is someone behind him) while the character has no idea, creating the anxiety of "turn around!". Storyboarding this needs 全景 or an over-the-shoulder shot so the audience sees the information outside the character's line of sight
- **Give time to look back after the reversal** — do not push the narrative forward immediately after a big reversal; leave 1-2 shots for the audience to digest it (the character frozen, an empty shot, silence) so the information settles

---

## 7. Designing character behaviour

- **Suspicious behaviour needs everyday clothing** — a character's suspicious behaviour must not be too obvious in the storyboard. "Checking the watch over and over" beats "glancing around furtively" — the first may be innocent, the second is too deliberate
- **The micro-expression is the main carrier of clues** — in mystery, a character's micro-expressions (pupils contracting slightly, a flicker at the corner of the mouth, fingers tapping the table lightly) are worth more than large performance. Where a clue-bearing expression is involved, catch it in 近景 (medium close-up) or 特写
- **Ambiguous action** — the character actions you design should permit several readings: a character wiping their hands may have just washed them, or may be wiping something off. The storyboard must not "explain" the meaning of the action with extra information; let the audience judge
- **No more than two actions in one shot** — "lowers the head to read the letter + folds it up quickly" is fine; "lowers the head to read the letter + folds it + puts it in the pocket + looks around" drowns the key action in information

---

## 8. Dialogue and air

- **The unfinished line is the best suspense** — "actually, that night… never mind." A character stopping mid-sentence is more unsettling than one who says it. In the storyboard, give this kind of line a 近景 plus silent air (2-3s)
- **One line, one shot** — avoid several lines of dialogue inside one shot, especially dialogue carrying key information. Cut the shot when the speaker changes
- **Support the ambiguous line with expression** — a line with a double meaning (normal on the surface, suspicious underneath) is paired with a subtle expression from the character; the storyboard uses 近景 to make sure the audience catches the detail of the expression
- **Keep narration and inner monologue restrained** — mystery narrative tells through the picture first; narration is used only when the inner chain of deduction must be stated. Put narration over a 中远景 (medium-long shot) or an empty shot, avoiding the mismatch of a 近景 with a mouth that does not move

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Hard cuts within a scene** — cut hard between shots inside one scene to keep it tight
- **Change scenes with a black frame or a white flash** — in mystery, use a short black frame (0.5-1s) between scenes to create a "break"; it fits the mystery rhythm better than an empty-shot transition
- **Enter flashbacks on a match cut** — cut from the present image into the memory image on a similar composition or action, strengthening the causal link (the character sees a knife now → flash back to the scene where that knife appeared)
- **Mark time jumps with a title** — a narrative that breaks the timeline needs an explicit time marker at the transition ("three days earlier", "at the same moment") so the audience does not get lost
- **End passages on a "suspense tail hook"** — leave one unresolved suspense shot at the end of each passage before the transition, keeping the pull across passages
