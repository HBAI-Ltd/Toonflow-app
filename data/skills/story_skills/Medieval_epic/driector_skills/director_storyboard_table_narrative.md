# Storyboard-Table Narrative Techniques · Medieval Epic · Technique Reference

---

## 1. Storyboard table positioning

The storyboard table is the director's core tool for turning script into shot language. Table fields are set by the director per project (shot number, scale, camera movement, duration, characters, event, dialogue, light, emotion, transition...); below is only the technique reference for the medieval epic genre.

---

## 2. Shot-scale choice

- **Wides are the epic's breath** — use wide / extreme-wide more than daily-life genres: the moor, the hall's height, the walls. They carry "the world's scale and one person's smallness", the root of the epic feel
- **Close-up density runs high** — a 30–50 minute film lives on faces: after every vast wide, place at least one close-up anchor. The scale jump "from the world to the eyes" is the genre's signature move
- **Medium shots carry the group scenes** — council sessions, tavern crowds, the guild circle need medium shots to state character relations and spatial positions
- **Extreme close-ups are reserved for fate moments** — the seal in a palm, the blade snapping, eyes before the decision, the bond's hand going still. Only at points where fate turns
- **Establishing shots must carry the world** — a gate scarred by old sieges tells more than a caption; every establishing frame answers "what kind of world is this"

---

## 3. Camera-movement rhythm

- **Slow is the default** — ≥70% of shots static or near-static; movement at the pace of consequence. Hurried camerawork breaks the genre's gravity
- **Slow push = fate focusing** — onto the accused's face, onto the seal, onto the door that will open. For key decisions and revelations
- **Slow pull = the world revealed** — from the lone figure out to the empty moor; from the two-shot out to the surrounding hunters. The classic "from person to fate" move
- **High slow drift = fate's gaze** — over the hunt, the burning refuge, the kneeling hall; history watching. Act turns only
- **The held stare = history as witness** — rites, sentences, deaths: absolute stillness; the static frame is itself the most solemn camera movement

---

## 4. Duration control

- **Wide / extreme-wide 5–7s** — let the audience feel the world's weight; never cut a wide short
- **Confrontation and dialogue close shots 4–5s** — dialogue tempo runs slower than daily drama; leave silence around lines so words land like stone
- **Ritual actions 3–5s each** — the seal struck away, the blade broken, the first knee down: every rite action gets full duration; solemnity comes from not hurrying
- **Close-ups 3–4s** — fate moments need to linger past comfort
- **Duels do not compress** — battle shots keep full lengths; tension escalates by scale change, not shortened cuts
- **Golden rule: gravity comes from not hurrying** — every rhythm runs half a beat slower than daily drama. Haste reads as lightness; patience reads as weight

---

## 5. Shot-merging strategy (de-AI-flavoring)

- **Group scenes in one moving master** — council sessions, tavern crowds, the guild circle: one slow drifting shot across the group beats fragmenting into per-face cuts
- **Merge pursuit and travel** — the hunt through the forest, the exile's road: one continuous tracking movement preserves the "flow" feeling; fragmenting kills momentum
- **Narrative density first** — every shot must advance the story or carry the genre's emotion; pure scenery merges into a shot that also works
- **Director-mind check** — after writing, ask: which of these would a period-drama director cover in one patient move? Merge those

---

## 6. One-shot long takes

- **When to use** — crossing the aftermath field, a complete rite, the walk from the hall doors to the high seat, the hero moving through the burning refuge. Value: "the audience stands inside history"
- **Typical setups** — through the guild hall as the council assembles; along the wall at first light; the walk to the kneeling: doors → aisle of former brothers → the seal held out
- **Notation** — write the full path in the camera field (e.g. "one-shot: high drift over the moor → descend beside the rider → track alongside → hold as he halts at the ridge"), and state opening and closing frames in the description
- **Duration extension** — long takes may exceed the 7s single-shot ceiling but stay ≤15s
- **Solemnity holds inside the take** — movement stays slow and weighted even through action; no hurried passages

---

## 7. Character interaction design

- **Confrontation is the genre's arena** — the strongest interactions are not fights: the stare across the council circle, the Huntmaster's silence, the former brother who will not meet his eyes. Stage confrontations at full duration with held frames
- **The bond's two-shots** — the relationship lives in shared frames at eye level, fire between them; trust growth = the two-shot tightening across the film; after the taking, repeat the same setups with one side empty
- **Body language is station** — who stands, who kneels, who sits the high seat; who turns their back; the kneeling scene inverts every prior arrangement, and the table should mark these inversions explicitly
- **The creature is a character** — after its reveal, cover the creature in the same grammar: eye-level frames, reaction close-ups on the eyes, two-shots with the hero

---

## 8. Dialogue and silence

- **Lines land like carved stone** — key dialogue in single short sentences; one oath outweighs a speech
- **Silence is punctuation** — mandatory quiet before and after key lines; the audience must "hear" the weight
- **Accent notation** — mark dialogue register per the planning skill: RP for council and nobility, regional UK for commoners and soldiers; delivery low and measured
- **Voiceover restraint** — at most an opening and a closing VO; never mid-film explanation

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Empty frames between worlds** — cut between story spaces through an unpeopled genre frame: the banner, the walls, the moor, crows on a fence; emotional buffer plus world texture
- **Time jumps through imagery** — snow over the old campfire, the healed scar, the repaired door; never date text
- **Slow fade between acts** — act boundaries take a slow fade to black and up, the rhythm of "history turning a page"
- **Hard cut as default** — within scenes, clean hard cuts keep the narration grave and unadorned

---

## 10. Safe violence staging (mandatory)

- **Impact off-frame** — strikes exit frame or land behind cover; the table's frame description holds the swing, the witness, or the aftermath — never the injury
- **The witness is the coverage** — mark the reaction shot as the primary shot of any violent beat: the bond watching, the line of brothers going still
- **Sparks, not blood** — contact language: "blades clash, sparks on impact, cinematic slow motion"; gore vocabulary is banned from every field of the table
- **Death by stillness** — "aftermath, quiet" shots: the hand releasing the sword, the empty helm, crows lifting, the riderless horse walking home
- **Aftermath fields** — torn banners, broken shields, grey snow, smoke; no bodies in explicit detail; assets referenced in these shots stay clean of blood
