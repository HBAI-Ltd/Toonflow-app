# Storyboard-table narrative technique · Psychological Drama · technique reference

---

## 1. What the storyboard table is for

The storyboard table is the director's core tool for turning the script into shot language. The fields of the form are set by the director as the project requires (shot number, shot size, camera movement, duration, characters, events, dialogue, lighting, emotion, transitions and so on); what follows is only a technique reference for the psychological drama narrative genre.

---

## 2. Choosing the shot size

- **特写 is the mind-reading tool** — 特写 (close-up) is used more often in psychological-drama narrative than in any other genre. 特写 is for catching the micro-expression tell where "what the mouth says and what the mind thinks do not match": the momentary drift of the eyes, an almost imperceptible lift at the corner of the mouth, the contraction of the pupil
- **中景 is the home ground of the game** — the workhorse shot sizes for a dialogue-game passage are 中景 (medium shot) and 近景 (medium close-up), making sure the audience sees the character's expression and body language at the same time. Pure 特写 loses the body information; pure 远景 loses the expression
- **远景 states the layout** — 远景 (long shot) is used to state the "layout" of the game: who is with whom, where the third party is, the power positions in the space
- **大特写 drops the clue** — 大特写 (extreme close-up) (a fingertip trembling, a change in the pupil, a twitch flickering across the corner of the mouth) is used to drop the key clue to the audience: this person is lying, this person has noticed something, this person is about to act
- **The establishing shot must hide information** — the establishing shot of a game narrative does not only state the space; it must already carry the properties of the "arena": enclosed or open, is there a third party, where the way out is

## 3. Camera-movement rhythm

- **静止 by default, 静止 is the standoff** — 65% or more of the shots should be on a 静止 (static) camera. In psychological drama, a 静止 camera carries the standoff of "two people are testing each other, and whoever moves first shows a tell first"
- **An extremely slow 缓推 = closing in / applying pressure** — slower than a normal 缓推 (slow push in), implying "the truth is being closed in on" or "one side is applying psychological pressure"
- **Slow pull back = revelation / discovery** — pulling slowly away from the character reveals the information the character does not have (someone behind them, the evidence on the table, another character's reaction)
- **The subtle drift = the scales tipping** — inside a 静止 frame the camera drifts extremely slowly to one side, implying the state of the game is changing
- **Fast cutting only for the reversal** — at the instant the cards are turned over, a fast jump through shot sizes is permitted (中景 → 特写 → 大特写) to simulate the shock to understanding. In every other passage, fast cutting is forbidden

---

## 4. Duration control

- **Dialogue shots 3-5s** — the shot-reverse-shot rhythm of game dialogue is slightly slower than everyday dialogue. The silent interval between lines is the psychological time in which "both sides are calculating"
- **Silent standoff 4-6s** — a passage of eye contact or silence with 无台词 (no dialogue) needs enough duration for the audience to feel "something is happening in the air"
- **Micro-expression 特写 2-3s** — the duration of a 特写 on a micro-expression tell must be exact: too short and the audience does not see it, too long and it becomes deliberate emphasis
- **The instant of the reversal 1-2s** — the key reaction shot as the cards turn over must be short and precise, fast enough that the audience is hit before it can react
- **远景 / stating the layout 3-4s** — a 远景 stating the layout of the game does not need to be long; as soon as the information lands it is done
- **Golden rule: the silence runs longer than the line** — in psychological drama, the silence between two lines should be longer than the lines themselves. Silence is the game played between the playing of cards

---

## 5. Shot-merging strategy (removing the AI feel)

- **Keep the shot-reverse-shot in dialogue passages** — unlike other genres, the dialogue passages of psychological drama need precise shot-reverse-shot to carry the rhythm of attack and defence and must not be forced together into a two-shot. Every switch is the ball being passed between the two people
- **Complete one round of the game as one group** — a complete "round of attack and defence" (probe → response → probe again → showdown) should be planned as one whole group of shots, keeping the internal rhythm continuous
- **Extremely high narrative density** — in psychological drama every shot must carry information (dialogue subtext, micro-expression, physical clue, spatial implication); pure atmosphere shots are not allowed
- **Check with the director's mind** — after writing the storyboard, check yourself: does every shot have an "information layer"? What can the audience "read" in this shot? If nothing can be read, this shot should not exist

---

## 6. One-take (merging into a long take)

- **When it applies** — two people changing position in a space, the power relation shifting with the position in space, moving from one state of information to another
- **Typical scenes** — character A stands and walks to the window (from equal to looking down), a character walks a circle around the table (a display of power), moving from the meeting room to the corridor (from public to private)
- **How to note it** — write the full path in the camera-movement field (for example "one take: character A stands up → 缓推 following them to the window → 摇镜 bringing in character B's reaction in their seat → settling on a two-person composition") and state the opening and closing frames in the shot description
- **Loosen the duration** — because the information density of a long take in this genre keeps updating, it may break the 6s ceiling for a single shot, but not exceed 12s
- **Movement in space is movement in psychology** — every step a character takes in space during a long take should correspond to a change in the state of the game

---

## 7. Designing character interaction

- **Dialogue is combat** — every line of game dialogue in the storyboard table should be annotated with its "subtext" or "real intent". The line is the surface; the subtext is the game
- **The micro-gesture is the tell** — every game shot in the storyboard table should give the character one small "real reaction" gesture: touching the ear absent-mindedly = nervous; tapping the table lightly = calculating; drinking water = buying time
- **Whoever speaks first loses first** — in a game standoff, silence is the weapon. The storyboard table should mark "whose silence it is" — who is producing the silence (actively applying pressure) and who is enduring it (defending passively)

---

## 8. Dialogue and air

- **Every line carries two meanings** — there is no "ordinary line" in psychological drama. The lines in the storyboard table should be spare and multivalent, so that on a second viewing the audience finds "so that is what that line meant"
- **Silence is more dangerous than dialogue** — the most dangerous moment in the game is not the argument but the moment one side suddenly stops talking. Silence means "I have already decided my next move"
- **The quantity of information in an answer** — the information in a line must be controlled exactly: answering too little = suspicious (hiding something); answering too much = suspicious (covering something); answering just right = the most suspicious of all (too perfect)

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Hard cut by default** — cut hard between shots inside one scene to keep the rhythm of the game clean and tense
- **A brief black frame between rounds** — from the end of one round of the game to the start of the next, a brief black frame of 0.3-0.5s can act as a "breath" while implying "a new round has begun"
- **Hard cut on a change of point of view** — when switching from character A's point of view to character B's, use a hard cut to create the impact of the flipped understanding
- **Flashbacks as flashes** — when a character in the game suddenly recalls a key clue, insert an extremely short flashback image (0.5-1s) to simulate the experience of a flash of understanding
- **Keep the tension across scene changes** — do not use a soft transition between different scenes; hold the tautness through the whole psychological drama. If a buffer is needed, use a short shot of a character alone (thinking, planning the next move) as the emotional transition
