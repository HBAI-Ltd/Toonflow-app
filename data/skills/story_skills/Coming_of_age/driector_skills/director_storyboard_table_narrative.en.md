# Storyboard-table narrative technique · Coming of Age · technique reference

---

## 1. What the storyboard table is for

The storyboard table is the director's core tool for turning the script into shot language. The fields of the form are set by the director as the project requires (shot number, shot size, camera movement, duration, characters, events, dialogue, lighting, emotion, transitions and so on); what follows is only a technique reference for the coming-of-age narrative genre.

---

## 2. Choosing the shot size

- **Ensemble shot sizes come first** — a large share of coming-of-age passages are ensemble scenes (class, break time, gatherings, competitions). 全景 (wide shot) and 中景 (medium shot) are the workhorses of an ensemble scene, keeping several people in frame to carry the belonging of "being together"
- **特写 catches the micro-expression** — much of the emotion in youth is carried by micro-expressions (a stifled smile, eyes going red, a nervous swallow). Place a 特写 (close-up) at the key emotional beats to catch these instants before they vanish
- **The freedom and loneliness of 远景** — 远景 (long shot) has a double function in coming-of-age narrative: 远景 of running on the sports field = freedom / energy; 远景 of the empty street after school = loneliness / reflection. Choose according to the emotional target
- **大特写 must feel like youth** — 大特写 (extreme close-up) — an ear tip gone red, a hand clenched tight, lashes blinking away tears — is for catching the moments of youth where "the mouth says nothing but the body is honest"
- **Establish with an everyday shot** — a coming-of-age establishing shot needs no grand empty vista; a desk with a sticky note on it, a chair with a sports shirt over the back, a corner piled with exam papers is enough

---

## 3. Camera-movement rhythm

- **Alternating movement and stillness** — 50% 静止 (static) camera + 30% tracking / 缓推 (slow push in) + 20% slight handheld shake. The camera rhythm of coming-of-age narrative should itself have the texture of "bouncing about one moment and going quiet the next"
- **Tracking a run** — tracking that follows a character running is the signature camera movement of coming-of-age narrative. Keep it slightly unsteady to carry the life force. Good for chasing a person, hurrying somewhere, sprinting in a race
- **缓推 = noticing / discovering** — the psychological cue that the audience (and the character) has noticed someone or something. Good for a crush being discovered, for noticing something is wrong with a friend, for seeing the graduation noticeboard
- **Slow pull back = leaving / letting go** — backing slowly away from the character or the scene; good for farewells, for graduation, for walking away alone
- **Slight handheld shake = the truth of everyday life** — use a light handheld shake in everyday ensemble passages to add the "being there" feeling and the documentary texture of "caught on the fly"

---

## 4. Duration control

- **Everyday fragments 2-3s** — the fragment shots of everyday fun cut fast, 2-3s each, using quantity and rhythm to carry "every day flying past"
- **Emotional passages stretched to 4-5s** — shots in emotional passages such as a flutter, an argument or a reconciliation should run 50% longer than everyday shots, giving the emotion time to ferment
- **Silent shots 3-4s** — a growth-realisation shot with 无台词 (the character standing still, watching someone walk away) needs enough duration for the audience to feel that "in this moment he/she grew up"
- **远景 / empty shots 4-6s** — seasonal and campus empty shots mark the passage of time; give them enough duration for the audience to "feel time moving"
- **Dialogue in 近景 3-4s** — coming-of-age dialogue runs fast, but the key lines (a confession, an argument, an apology) should be slowed appropriately
- **The golden rule: thickness is built out of fragments** — the emotional thickness of coming-of-age narrative does not come from one long take, it comes from the accumulation of many fragments. Better a few more 2-3s fragments than one dragging long take

---

## 5. Shot-merging strategy (removing the AI feel)

- **Keep the fragmented cutting of everyday passages** — unlike other genres, the fragmented cutting of everyday-fun passages in coming-of-age narrative (fast-switching everyday snippets) is itself the narrative technique and does not need to be forcibly merged
- **Complete an emotional passage in one shot** — key emotional passages (a confession, an argument, a reconciliation) should use a complete long take rather than fragmented cutting, giving the emotion room to develop continuously
- **Cover the ensemble in one shot** — when a multi-person ensemble scene can be covered by one shot with camera movement, do not split it into fragmented cuts of one person at a time. Hold the wholeness of "we are together"
- **The director's-thinking test** — once the storyboard is written, self-check: do the everyday passages feel fragmented (yes = right)? Do the emotional passages feel continuous (yes = right)? Do the two rhythms contrast (yes = right)?

---

## 6. One-take (merging into a long take)

- **When it applies** — a character crossing the campus, a chase or a run, the change of mood in going from one space to another. The value of a long take in coming-of-age narrative is "living through this moment alongside this person"
- **Typical scenes** — a character running down the corridor towards the sports field, running after a bus, walking through the classroom emptied out after graduation, looking for one particular person in a crowd
- **How to mark it** — state the full path in the camera movement field (for example "一镜到底：track the character bursting out of the classroom → through the corridor → pushing the door open onto the sports field → close on the character stopping and looking back"), and give the opening and closing framings in the shot description
- **Relaxed duration** — because the following is continuous and the information keeps renewing, it may exceed the 6s single-shot limit, but not 12s
- **Emotional continuity** — the emotion inside a long take should have an arc (from anxious to released / from happy to deflated); do not let a long take become a flat stretch of emotion

---

## 7. Designing character interaction

- **Ensemble interaction needs layers** — in a scene of several people horsing about, each of them should have their own small action or expression; do not have everyone do the same reaction. Layering = truth
- **Coming-of-age interaction runs on clumsiness** — a hand touched and pulled back, wanting to speak and closing the mouth, deliberately taking the other road and stealing a glance anyway — the core of youthful interaction is "wanting to get closer and not knowing how"
- **Physical distance is the relationship** — mark the changing distance between characters in the storyboard table: a seat apart while still strangers, shoulder to shoulder once familiar, standing on opposite sides after a fight, leaning together again after making up

---

## 8. Dialogue and the space around it

- **Coming-of-age dialogue should be short and fragmentary** — conversation in youth is fragmentary: a half sentence cut off, a sentence changed halfway, the mouth saying "it's nothing" while the face gives everything away
- **The important things cannot be said** — the most powerful dialogue in coming-of-age narrative is often "the words that could not be said". Use silence, a turned back, pretending it is nothing, instead of stating it plainly
- **Overlapping voices in ensemble scenes** — in a coming-of-age ensemble, several people talk at once, cut across each other, all clamouring — do not aim for every line to be audible; the mess is itself the sound of youth

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Cut everyday passages as a fast montage** — for the everyday passages that say "every day is much the same and every day is precious", use a fast hard-cut montage of fragments: class → break → home time → class → break…
- **Use an empty shot to bridge an emotional turn** — when the emotion changes sharply, insert a seasonal or campus empty shot as an emotional buffer
- **Use a contrast transition for a time jump** — the same scene at the same angle, but with the details changed (different writing on the blackboard / a different person in the seat / the leaves a different colour); the hard-cut contrast carries the passage of time
- **Hard cut by default** — shots within the same scene are joined by hard cuts, keeping the brightness of youth
