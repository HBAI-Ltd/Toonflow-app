# Storyboard-table narrative technique · Urban Workplace Drama · technique reference

---

## 1. What the storyboard table is for

The storyboard table is the director's core tool for turning the script into shot language. The fields of the form are set by the director as the project requires (shot number, shot size, camera movement, duration, characters, events, dialogue, lighting, emotion, transitions and so on); what follows is only a technique reference for the urban workplace narrative genre.

---

## 2. Choosing the shot size

- **Shot size is social distance** — the core logic of shot size in a workplace storyboard is "the sense of distance between people". Use 中景 (medium shot) for formal occasions (social distance) and only go into 近景 (medium close-up) for a confrontation or an intimacy
- **中景 is the workhorse shot size** — 中景 is used most often in workplace narrative (40-50%), because the workplace is itself a social field of "keeping an appropriate distance"
- **近景 breaks the distance** — in the workplace, 近景 implies that "the distance has been broken": it may be trust (a private confidence) or it may be pressure (a superior closing in). Every use of 近景 means the relationship has changed
- **特写 catches the subtext** — everyone in the workplace wears a mask and the real emotion hides in the micro-expression. The core function of 特写 (close-up) is "tearing the mask off": the tension under a restrained smile, the contempt inside a nod of agreement, the hesitation while signing
- **The establishing shot states the space of power** — a workplace establishing shot is not just "this is an office"; it must state the implications of power in the space: who sits at the head, who is near the door, whether the space is open or closed. Get it done in 1-2 shots

---

## 3. Camera-movement rhythm

- **Mostly a 固定 camera** — 65% or more of the shots are 固定 (locked off), carrying a cool, objective observing point of view
- **Documentary handheld** — use an extremely slight handheld when following a character walking or commuting; it adds authenticity while the shake stays almost imperceptible
- **缓推 = pressure / scrutiny** — 缓推 (slow push in) towards a character's face; it suits passages of being questioned, making a decision, bearing pressure
- **Slow pull back = isolation / the whole picture** — pulling from the character out to 全景 (wide shot), revealing how small the individual is inside a vast system
- **Lateral move = scanning the ensemble** — a horizontal lateral move sweeping across several characters or desks, establishing the group relations and the whole of the space
- **Camera movement is attitude** — 固定 = cool observation from outside; 缓推 = pressing in / attention; slow pull back = detachment / isolation; handheld = real participation

---

## 4. Duration control

- **The standard rhythm of dialogue shots** — a shot with dialogue runs 2-4s, one line per cut, holding the rhythm of the conversation and the clarity of the information
- **Stretch the silences of a manoeuvre** — give a silent shot inside a stand-off or a manoeuvre 3-4s, so the silence itself becomes the weapon. The audience can feel "the thing that was not said"
- **Everyday fragments cut fast** — everyday snippets such as the commute, typing or drinking coffee need only 1.5-2s each; the fast fragmentation carries the rhythm of everyday life
- **Give the shots alone their duration** — sitting alone in the car, washing the face late at night, standing at the window — give these "real instants" 4-5s, so the audience feels the exhaustion of a character with the mask taken off
- **The golden 6-second rule** — no single shot over 6s. The power of workplace narrative lies in information density, not duration; better to cut in one more angle than to hold one shot to death
- **Montage fragments** — the fragment shots of a passage-of-time passage run 1-1.5s each, stacking fast to carry the pressure of "day after day"

---

## 5. Designing the dialogue storyboard

- **Cutting between positions of power** — in a dialogue storyboard, who sits high or low in frame implies the power relation. A low angle on the superior plus a high angle on the subordinate is the classic power intercut
- **The power implication of the over-the-shoulder** — in an over-the-shoulder shot, "whose shoulder" appears in the foreground = who holds the floor right now. Use the other party's shoulder as the foreground occlusion for the character being suppressed
- **Triangular composition** — use a triangular composition for a three-way manoeuvre, with the three of them at three positions in frame. Adjust the triangle dynamically as the alignments shift
- **Carry subtext with an expression shot** — after a line that carries a second meaning, cut to a 1-2s reaction shot of the listener (近景 / 特写), so the audience reads the subtext off the listener's micro-expression
- **Escalate from 全景 to 近景 in the meeting room** — a meeting passage starts in 全景 (the whole picture) and escalates towards 近景 / 特写 (focusing on the two sides of the conflict) as the conflict rises; the escalation of shot size maps the escalation of tension
- **Do not split into two shots what one shot can state** — if one shot with a lateral move can show the speaker and the listener's reaction at the same time, do not split it into two shots of shot and reverse

---

## 6. Passages alone

- **The truth after work** — leaning against the wall of the lift with eyes closed, the silence of driving home alone, collapsing onto the sofa on getting in — these passages are the most precious "real moments" of workplace narrative; use a 中远景 (medium-long shot) on a 固定 camera and give them plenty of duration
- **The restraint of breaking down alone** — a working person does not break down by wailing; they break down in a deep breath in the toilets, in red-rimmed eyes, in a hand pressed to the forehead. Catch the restrained breakdown in 近景; it resonates far more than an exaggerated performance
- **Reflection late at night** — pair the reflection or the looking back of being alone at night with 远景 (long shot) plus an empty environment shot; the city lights outside the window imply that "there are countless equally exhausted people in this city"
- **The narrative function of the commute** — the commute is not a connector, it is narrative: the exhausted faces on the underground, the resignation in a traffic jam, the deep breath before walking into the company — these instants of "switching persona" are the truest ritual of a working person

---

## 7. Dialogue and subtext

- **A second meaning is the norm** — workplace dialogue rarely means what it says. "This proposal could be optimised further" = no; "I respect your decision" = I disagree but I do not want a fight. The storyboard must use expression shots to help the audience read the subtext
- **The attack and defence of silence** — the silence after a question is asked, the silence of waiting for an answer after a proposal — silence in workplace dialogue is the tactic of "forcing the other side to speak first". Give the silence its own 2-3s shot
- **One line, one shot** — a key manoeuvring line takes a shot of its own and does not share it with other dialogue. Give that line a 近景 plus the listener's reaction
- **Narration and inner monologue almost never** — workplace narrative runs on the contrast of "words against deeds" (the mouth saying "it's fine" while the face says otherwise), not on narration explaining the interior. Use narration only where information must be supplied
- **Managing focus in a group conversation** — in a meeting of several people you do not need to cut a shot for every person who speaks. Use 全景 / 中景 to show the whole and cut to 近景 only for the key statements

---

## 8. Transition design

- **Hard cut within the same scene** — hard cuts between shots inside the same meeting room or office, keeping the rhythm clean
- **Use a fragment montage for everyday flow** — for the day-after-day of workplace routine, use a fast fragment montage (alarm → commute → clocking in → meeting → overtime → home), 1-1.5s per fragment
- **Contrasting-space transition** — hard cut from the busy office to the quiet of home late at night, using the spatial contrast to carry the split between "two worlds"
- **Use a caption or an empty shot for a time jump** — for a large time gap, mark it with a caption ("Three months later") or imply it with an empty shot of a change of season or weather
- **Bridge passages with the commute** — the commute is workplace narrative's natural passage divider: the commute in = a new passage beginning; the commute home = a passage closing
- **Phone-screen transition** — a notification, an email or a news push as the transition trigger; the picture focuses on the phone screen → the new information → the new passage is introduced
